a sauceBone Suckin' Sauce

also: Bone Suckin' Sweet Southern · Ford's Foods sauce

Bone Suckin', with the g dropped on the label.

A Raleigh line of bottled sauces, of which the one that built the brand is Sweet Southern: cane sugar first, tomato puree second, water third, apple cider vinegar fourth, at six grams of sugar to the tablespoon. The house also bottles an Eastern Vinegar sauce, whose label puts apple cider vinegar and distilled vinegar first and tomato paste sixth, and a mustard whose seven ingredients name no tomato at all. One maker, three readings of the same state's shelf.

The story Cited · web-bonesuckin-eastern-nc-page

The company dates the sauce to Raleigh in 1992 and calls the recipe a family one; the Sweet Southern page describes that bottle as the flavor the brand launched with. The mustard's page quotes Mr. Ford, of Ford's Foods, on making a sweet version of the house's sweet-and-spicy mustard without the heat.

The interesting part is on the backs of the bottles. Sweet Southern reads sugar, tomato, water, vinegar — the order of a Kansas City sauce, made in the eastern half of North Carolina, where the sauce a pit puts on a chopped hog has neither sugar nor tomato in it. The Eastern Vinegar bottle, sold alongside it, inverts that: two vinegars, then water, then cane sugar, with tomato paste down at six. The maker's own page for that bottle describes the tomato and molasses in it as a light hint behind the vinegar. Inference — a house that sells both is not choosing between the styles; it is selling the distance between them as two products.

Sauce for Barbecues (receipt 335)

Mrs. A. P. Hill · Mrs. Hill's New Cook-Book · 1867 · p. 171 · source · Public domain
Melt half a pound of butter; stir into it a large tablespoonful of mustard, half a teaspoonful of red pepper, one of black, salt to taste; add vinegar until the sauce has a strong acid taste. The quantity of vinegar will depend upon the strength of it. As soon as the meat becomes hot, begin to baste, and continue basting frequently until it is done; pour over the meat any sauce that remains.

The Southern mop of 1867 that pre-dates any tomato in a barbecue bottle.
