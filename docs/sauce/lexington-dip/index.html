a sauceLexington dip

also: dip · Lexington-style sauce · Piedmont sauce · Western sauce · red sauce · barbecue dip

'Dip' is the Piedmont's word; a Lexington counter will not correct you for 'sauce', but the menu says dip.

The Piedmont's sauce: the east's vinegar, salt, pepper and red pepper with ketchup or tomato and water added, sometimes sugar, so that it comes out thin and reddish — tangy, a little sweet or hot by the house, and every restaurant's recipe its own. It is the sauce of pork-shoulder barbecue in Lexington, Greensboro, Salisbury, Shelby and the rest of the Piedmont. It is basted on the shoulders as they cook, goes on the chopped meat at the block, stands on the table, and, in place of mayonnaise, dresses the red slaw.

Root. English dip (n.), from the verb dip, Old English dyppan, Proto-Germanic daupejanan, a causative of deup- 'deep'. The noun 'act of dipping' is recorded from the 1590s and the sense 'a liquid into which something is dipped' by 1825.
First seen. the noun sense 'liquid into which something is dipped' by 1825 (etymonline, dip n.1); no dated first use of 'dip' for the Lexington sauce found by this project
Tradition holds — the name is from the use: the chopped meat is dipped in it, and it is thin enough to dip into rather than spread. Etymonline notes that the sense 'thick, savory sauce for dunking pieces of raw vegetables' (by 1962) is a modern re-coinage, so the barbecue dip keeps the older, thinner sense. Cited · etymonline

The story Cited · web-ourstate-lexington-cover-up

The dip came out of the Lexington tents. Sid Weaver sold barbecue by the old courthouse from 1919 and Jess Swicegood beside him; Warner Stamey learned from both and carried the sauce to Shelby and Greensboro, and the men he taught — Wayne Monk at Lexington Barbecue among them — carried it further. Tradition holds — the tomato came in with cheap ketchup after the First World War, the same years Wikipedia's sauce history gives for ketchup and sugar entering barbecue sauces generally; the South Carolina Barbeque Association dates the same vinegar-and-ketchup idea to about 1900 in the Pee Dee and the North Carolina Piedmont.

The ketchup is what the columnists fought over. Jerry Bledsoe of the Greensboro Daily News took the Piedmont's side — 'America loves ketchup' — and Dennis Rogers of the Raleigh News & Observer the east's: 'People who would put ketchup in the sauce they feed to innocent children are capable of most anything.' The joke ran for decades and reached the General Assembly over the Lexington Barbecue Festival's title in 2005–07. In Lexington the sauce is also the slaw dressing: red slaw is cabbage, vinegar, ketchup and black pepper, no mayonnaise, and the Southern Foodways Alliance's note on Bar-B-Q Center says it plainly — in Lexington, barbecue eaters don't squirt sauce, they dip.

How it is done Cited · wp-lexington-north-carolina

Vinegar, ketchup, water, salt, pepper and other spices, in the town's own description; red pepper flakes and sugar in many kitchens; simmered and kept warm. On the pit it is a baste for the shoulders through a ten-hour cook. At the block it is spooned into the chopped meat; at the table it comes in a bottle. For red slaw, chopped cabbage is dressed with it — the same vinegar, ketchup and pepper — instead of mayonnaise, so the slaw matches the meat.

Today Cited · wp-lexington-north-carolina

Every Lexington pit keeps its own recipe; the town's Wikipedia article calls each one 'a closely guarded secret'. Red slaw dressed with it goes on hot dogs across the Piedmont as well as on trays.

Eastern North Carolina Barbecue Sauce

Wikibooks Cookbook · read 16 September 2026 · source · CC BY-SA 4.0
  • ¾ gallons apple cider vinegar
  • ⅛ cup salt
  • ¼ cup ground black pepper
  • ¼ cup red pepper flakes
  • 1 cup molasses
  • 2 cups honey
1. Start with apple cider vinegar in saucepan over medium heat. 2. Add salt, ground black pepper, crushed red pepper, molasses, and honey. 3. Let mixture heat up, but not quite boil. 4. Mix well. 5. Return sauce to the jug that the vinegar came in to cool.

Makes ¾ gallon.

The base the dip is built on: to make a Piedmont dip from it, a house adds ketchup or tomato and water and cuts the pepper back.

The dip, as described

source · ingredients-only
  • vinegar

Barbecue Sauce (Sweet)

Wikibooks Cookbook · read 16 September 2026 · source · CC BY-SA 4.0
  • 1 cup tomato catsup (ketchup)
  • ¾ cup cider vinegar
  • 2–3 tablespoons mustard
  • 3 tablespoons Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 cup brown sugar
  • 1–2 teaspoons celery seed
  • 3 tablespoons margarine
  • ¼ teaspoon black pepper
  • ¼ teaspoon red pepper
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon onion flakes
  • Garlic powder or minced garlic to taste (optional)
1. Combine all ingredients together and bring to a boil. Reduce heat and simmer 10 to 15 minutes, until it thickens slightly. 2. Arrange chicken pieces or pork chops in a greased baking dish. 3. Pour half of sauce over and bake at 350°F (175°C) for 30 to 40 minutes. 4. Pour remaining sauce over before serving.

Thicker and sweeter than a dip — a cup of ketchup to three quarters of a cup of cider vinegar, with brown sugar, mustard and Worcestershire. A dip runs the other way: mostly vinegar, with the ketchup a colouring.

Sauce for Barbecues (receipt 335)

Mrs. A. P. Hill · Mrs. Hill's New Cook-Book · 1867 · p. 171 · source · Public domain
Melt half a pound of butter; stir into it a large tablespoonful of mustard, half a teaspoonful of red pepper, one of black, salt to taste; add vinegar until the sauce has a strong acid taste. The quantity of vinegar will depend upon the strength of it. As soon as the meat becomes hot, begin to baste, and continue basting frequently until it is done; pour over the meat any sauce that remains.

An ancestor rather than the thing: the vinegar-and-pepper mop that the Piedmont later reddened. No tomato in it.

Not to be confused with

  • vinegar-and-pepper sauce — Any red in the bottle is dip; the east's sauce is clear. On the tray, red slaw beside the meat settles it.
  • heavy tomato sauce — Dip pours like vinegar and soaks in; a thick sauce that coats the meat is the grocery-shelf kind, not dip.
