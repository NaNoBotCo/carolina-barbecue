the pitblock pit

also: cinder-block pit · concrete-block pit · open pit · pig pit

An open pit built above ground of cinder blocks — concrete blocks — wide and shallow, a little longer than a split hog, with a rack across the top on which the pig lies and a bed of charcoal below; brick or steel are the alternatives, and the charcoal is made in a fireplace or a burn barrel beside it. It is the whole-hog pit of the Carolinas, the one a farm, a fire department or a family can lay in an afternoon. *Tradition holds —* the blocks go up dry, without mortar, two or three courses high, a gap is left for the shovel, and a sheet of tin or plywood closes the top.

The story Cited · wp-whole-hog-barbecue

The trench came first. Tradition holds — the farm barbecue of the Carolinas was cooked over a pit dug in the ground, and Mallard Creek Presbyterian Church in Charlotte cooked its 1929 barbecue that way, hickory coals shoved under whole hogs in ditches; by the 1980s metal cookers had replaced the church's dirt pits, with some meat still cooked on open pits inside a screened building. NCpedia records that few commercial pits today are dug in the ground. The cinder block — cheap, fireproof, stackable — is what the open pit became when it climbed out of the ditch. It is the pit of the pig pickin' and the cook-off, and the pit the whole-hog restaurants of the east grew from: the Skylight Inn's hogs cook over charcoal made in fireplaces beside the pits, and Rodney Scott's over hardwood charcoal shoveled from a burn barrel. The Cuban roasting box, by contrast, is a closed box of concrete blocks and steel mesh with the coals on top.

How it is done Tradition

Blocks are laid in a rectangle a little longer and wider than the split hog; a rack spans the top, the hog goes on skin-side up, and a lid holds the heat. Coals from the burn barrel are shoveled in and spread beneath the carcass on a regular round through the night — Tradition holds — heavier under the hams and shoulders, thinner under the loin. The fat that drips hisses on the coals, and a flare-up is the danger the cook watches for; an uncontrolled pit fire can take the pit and the building. After eight to twenty-four hours the hog is flipped once, skin down, to crisp.

Today Tradition

The block pit is the pit of the yard, the church lot, the fire station and the competition; restaurants that cook whole hog mostly cook in masonry or steel pits under a roof, and the form — a bed of coals, a rack, a hog, a lid — is the same.

Not to be confused with

  • brick pit — A block pit is open, unmortared and sized to a whole hog; a brick pit is a closed chamber with a door and a chimney, built for shoulders.
  • pig cooker — A block pit is stacked masonry on the ground; a pig cooker is a steel box on a trailer.
