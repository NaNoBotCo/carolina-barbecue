also: barbecue pit · the pit · pit house · open pit

'The pit' is the brick or block box the coals go in, the room it sits in, and often the whole business: 'out back at the pit', 'the pit opens at ten'.

The place the cooking happens. First a trench in the ground filled with hardwood coals, the split hog on a rack above; then a wide, shallow container of cinder block, brick or steel above ground, still a pit; then, at many places, a gas cooker, still the pit. The word also names the room, the building — a pit house — and the restaurant, as in Backyard BBQ Pit or Dickey's Barbecue Pit. A smokehouse is a different building: a windowless outbuilding where hams and bacon are cured in cold smoke for weeks, not a place a meal is cooked.

Root. Old English pytt 'a hole in the ground, water hole, well, grave', a Germanic borrowing of Latin puteus 'well, pit, shaft'; the barbecue sense keeps the hole's name after the hole has become a box of brick or cinder block above ground, and then a gas cooker inside a building
First seen. Old English (etymonline); the barbecue-pit sense is undated in the dictionaries consulted — Wiktionary lists two dozen senses of pit and none for barbecue
Wikipedia's article on pit barbecue says the word is applied across the 'barbecue belt' to an enclosed, above-ground pit; NCpedia says few commercial barbecue pits today are dug in the ground. Cited · etymonline

The story Cited · wp-whole-hog-barbecue

Pit is a Germanic borrowing of Latin puteus, a well; in Old English a hole, a well, a grave and hell. The barbecue pit was that hole: Wikipedia's article on pit barbecue describes meat cooked buried below ground, an earth-oven practice of Indigenous peoples. The whole-hog article has the pit dug into the earth or built above it as a wide, shallow container, usually of cinder blocks, filled with charcoal burned down from wood in a fireplace or a burn barrel; the South Carolina Encyclopedia gives the traditional pit as an open pit fuelled by hardwood coals in a rectangular cement-block structure, the meat suspended above. NCpedia says few commercial pits today are dug in the ground; the whole-hog article says new restaurants mostly use gas-fired pits and many older ones have switched. At the Skylight Inn the pit crew makes its charcoal from oak and hickory logs in fireplaces beside the pits. Ed Mitchell co-founded a Raleigh restaurant called The Pit. Inference — a word that survived the loss of the hole survived the loss of the fire: the pit is wherever the barbecue is made, and the name on the sign says so.

Not to be confused with

  • gas and electric cookers — A cooker is an appliance; a pit is a place. When a pit switches to gas the appliance sits inside the place and the sign does not change.
  • pig cooker — A pig cooker is towed to the party; a pit stays where it was built.
