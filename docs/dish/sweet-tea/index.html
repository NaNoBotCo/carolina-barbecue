'Tea' on a Carolina barbecue menu means this: iced, and sweetened while hot. Lexington Barbecue prints it as 'Iced Tea (Unsweet on Request)'; the unsweetened glass is the one you ask for.

Black tea brewed strong, sweetened with sugar while hot — so it dissolves, rather than being stirred into a cold glass — and poured over ice. It is the drink of the Carolina barbecue house in both states and every sauce region: sold by the glass, the quart and the gallon, brewed on the stove at Grady's in Dudley, named by Dukes in Orangeburg among the five things people come for, and served, Wikipedia says, wherever whole hog is. Some cooks temper it with a pinch of baking soda against bitterness; lemon is the usual addition. Its presence on a menu is one sign used to tell whether a place counts as the South.

The story Cited · web-moss-sweet-tea-summerville

Sweet tea began as a luxury of three shipped-in things — tea, sugar and ice, the ice the dearest — and became cheap when all three did. The oldest known recipe for it is in Housekeeping in Old Virginia, a community cookbook compiled by Marion Cabell Tyree (copyright 1878, and dated 1879 by the historian Robert Moss): ice, sugar, hot green tea, lemon. Green tea was the tea of the 19th-century South; the Second World War cut off its sources and black tea from British India took its place, and by the war's end black tea was the American tea. Moss, checking Summerville, South Carolina's 2013 claim to be 'The Birthplace of Sweet Tea' — a trademarked tagline, a Sweet Tea Trail and billboards on I-26 and I-95, resting on Dr. Charles Shepard's Pinehurst Tea Plantation of 1888–1915, the first successful tea farm in the country — found the Boston Journal recommending iced tea in July 1868, twenty years before Shepard planted, and Fannie Farmer's 1896 Boston Cooking-School Cook Book telling readers to pour tea over cracked ice and sweeten to taste. Summerville answered with a jar: 'Mason', fifteen feet tall, filled on National Tea Day 2016 with 2,524 gallons of sweet tea, the world's largest, a record traded since 2010 among Chick-fil-A, Summerville and Lipton. In 2003 a Georgia House bill, apparently an April Fool's joke, would have made selling iced tea without offering sweet tea a misdemeanor; it never came to a vote.

How it is done Cited · wp-sweet-tea

Tea bags or loose black tea steep in a pot of boiling water — some cooks add a pinch of baking soda — and the sugar goes in while the tea is hot; cold water and ice bring it down. At Grady's, Gerri Grady brews it on the stovetop for the day's service; at Allen & Son Keith Allen brewed it himself and sold it by the litre. It is served in a tall glass over ice, with lemon if you want it. Pits sell it to go by the pint, quart, half gallon and gallon — Parker's in Wilson lists all four — and the Barbecue Center in Lexington prices it from a 10-ounce cup to the gallon jug. The sugar can run to 22 degrees Brix, twice a Coca-Cola.

Iced Tea

Emma and William McKinney · Aunt Caroline's Dixieland Recipes · 1922 · p. 25 · source · Public domain
Use one scant teaspoonful of tea to one cup of boiling water. Pour boiling water over tea leaves and let stand until milk warm. Then strain and sweeten to taste while tea is still warm, as it requires less sugar. Serve with crushed ice, green mint leaves, and sliced lemon and orange.

The sweetening rule the Carolina glass still follows, printed in 1922: sugar goes in while the tea is warm, because warm tea takes less of it.

Tea — Southern Style

Emma and William McKinney · Aunt Caroline's Dixieland Recipes · 1922 · p. 25 · source · Public domain
Take one-half teaspoonful of tea to one cup of boiling water. Put the tea in the pot, pour the boiling water upon it and let stand where it will keep hot for five minutes. Then serve. Tea should never be boiled for it makes it bitter.

The hot brew on the facing column, at half the strength of the iced one: iced tea is made stronger because the ice waters it down.

Iced Tea

Wikibooks Cookbook · read 16 September 2026 · source · CC BY-SA 4.0
  • 3 tea bags
  • 1 teaspoon stevia
  • 2 teaspoons citric acid crystals (or malic / tartaric / citric blends)
  • 4 teaspoons sugar
1. Fill a drip coffee maker with cold water and place the tea bags in the basket. 2. Place the stevia, acid, and sugar in the pot, and place the pot in the machine. 3. Let it run through its cycle, then chill the product. This is a concentrate and will make several litres of iced tea. 4. Place some ice in a tall glass and fill 80–90% with cold water. Pour in some iced tea nectar. You may reduce the water and add some fruit juice if desired.

Makes more than 24 servings.

A concentrate brewed through a drip coffee maker and diluted in the glass; sweetened with stevia and a little sugar rather than the Carolina cup of it.
