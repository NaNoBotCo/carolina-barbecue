Carolina BarbecueThe pig

Every argument here comes down to how much of the hog goes on the fire. Here's the hog, and here's what each style takes off it.

The hog, divided

Side view, facing left. A pit that cooks the whole hog uses all of this at once; a pit that cooks shoulders uses one block of it.

jowlthe head, the cheeks and the snout — whole-hog country only
shoulderthe Boston butt on top and the picnic below: the Lexington cut
loin and ribsthe long muscle along the back, and the ribs under it
bellythe side, which is bacon anywhere else
hamthe back leg, which Midlands pits cook by itself

The cuts, in their own words

whole hog

The entire dressed pig — dressed meaning gutted and scraped of hair, the head sometimes left on — split open along the backbone, laid flat over coals and cooked as one pi

pork shoulder

The front leg and shoulder of the pig, sold as one primal — a primal being one of the large first divisions of a carcass — or split in two: the butt, the upper half with

outside brown

The dark crust from the outside of a pork shoulder — the bark, where ten hours of smoke and dip have cooked into the surface — ordered by name at Lexington pits.

pork skins

The pig's hide, cooked until it blisters and cracks. On an eastern whole hog the skin faces up through the night and is turned to the coals at the end to crisp; at Skylig

hash and rice

A thick stew, or gravy, of pork cooked down with onions until it can be eaten with a spoon, ladled over white rice beside the barbecue.

The drawing is this project's own, and it is a diagram rather than a butcher's chart: the lines are where the styles differ, not where a knife goes. For the actual seams, a pork cutting chart from a state extension service is the thing to read.
